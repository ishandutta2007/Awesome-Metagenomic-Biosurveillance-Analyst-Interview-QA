# Topic 12: Field Trajectory & Public Health Integration

## Overview
Global surveillance network development, One Health biosurveillance integration, the path from research demonstration to operational public health infrastructure, and the realistic trajectory of metagenomic biosurveillance's role in global health security.

---

### Q1: What is the "One Health" framework and how does it shape metagenomic biosurveillance program design, particularly for zoonotic disease surveillance?

**A:**
**One Health framework:**
One Health is a collaborative, multisectoral approach recognizing that human health, animal health, and environmental health are inextricably interconnected — the majority of emerging infectious diseases affecting humans are zoonotic (originating in animals), and effective biosurveillance requires monitoring at the animal-human-environment interface rather than only in human clinical settings after disease has already crossed species barriers. The WHO, FAO, and OIE (now WOAH) formally endorsed One Health as the framework for integrated global health security in the Tripartite Zoonoses Guide and subsequent Global Health Security Agenda commitments.

**How One Health shapes metagenomic biosurveillance program design:**

1. **Surveillance at the zoonotic spillover interface, not just after human cases:**
   The most valuable biosurveillance intervention is detecting potentially dangerous pathogens in animal reservoir populations before they cause human disease — this requires wildlife and livestock surveillance programs (often using metagenomic approaches given that many reservoir hosts can't be individually tested) in the geographic and ecological zones where spillover risk is highest. Zoonotic spillover hotspots (tropical forest margins where wildlife-human interface is high; live animal markets; slaughterhouses; regions with high bat or rodent diversity) should be prioritized for proactive surveillance even before any human cases are detected.

2. **Data sharing and joint analysis across human-animal-environmental domains:**
   One Health surveillance requires connecting data streams that are traditionally siloed in separate institutional authorities (human health: ministries of health, clinical laboratories; animal health: agriculture ministries, veterinary services; environment: wildlife agencies). Metagenomic biosurveillance programs with One Health scope must establish data sharing agreements and analytical frameworks enabling joint analysis of sequences from human clinical cases, animal surveillance, and environmental samples — enabling identification of spillover events and transmission chains that cross sectoral boundaries.

3. **Integrated laboratory network design:**
   Building laboratory capacity for metagenomic surveillance in resource-limited settings where zoonotic spillover risk is highest requires integrated network design across human, animal, and environmental laboratory sectors — sharing sequencing infrastructure, bioinformatics capacity, and expertise across previously siloed laboratory systems rather than building separate parallel capabilities for each sector.

4. **Epidemiological investigation capacity spanning sectors:**
   When a potential zoonotic emergence is detected (a human case of an unusual respiratory illness at the same time as unusual mortality in nearby livestock), the investigative response requires teams with both human epidemiology and veterinary epidemiology expertise conducting joint field investigation — with metagenomic sampling of both the human cases and the relevant animal populations to establish whether genomic linkage supports zoonotic origin.

**Key One Health biosurveillance programs and their lessons:**

- **PREDICT program (USAID):** Largest systematic global wildlife surveillance program for zoonotic viruses — discovered 1,100+ novel viruses in wildlife across 31 countries using metagenomics; established a model for systematic spillover-zone surveillance
- **MOSAIC (Mapping Outbreak Situations and Abnormal Illness or Conditions):** Integrated syndromic surveillance across human-animal interface
- **SEEG (Spatial Epidemiology of Emerging pathogens in Gorillas) and related programs:** Species-specific surveillance at high-risk human-wildlife interfaces

### Q2: What is the realistic 5-10 year trajectory for metagenomic biosurveillance integration into routine public health infrastructure, and what are the key barriers and enabling conditions for this transition?

**A:**
**Realistic 5-10 year trajectory:**

**Near-term (1-3 years) — Consolidation and standardization:**
The primary near-term challenge is not expanding metagenomic surveillance capabilities but standardizing and institutionalizing what already exists from COVID-19 pandemic investments. Many countries and jurisdictions built wastewater and/or genomic surveillance infrastructure during COVID-19 that now risk being dismantled as pandemic emergency funding expires. Key near-term developments:
- Wastewater surveillance transition from emergency to routine public health infrastructure (ongoing in US, EU, Australia; uncertain in low-income countries)
- International standardization of surveillance data formats enabling cross-country comparison (WHO/CDC joint surveillance data standards development)
- Integration of pathogen genomic sequencing into routine laboratory diagnostic workflows at public health laboratories
- Development and publication of validated clinical mNGS standards enabling broader clinical adoption

**Medium-term (3-7 years) — Expanded pathogen portfolios and network integration:**
- Multi-pathogen wastewater surveillance programs routinely monitoring influenza, RSV, norovirus, enteroviruses, and AMR alongside SARS-CoV-2 becoming standard in high-income countries
- Global genomic surveillance networks (building on GISAID model) expanded beyond influenza and SARS-CoV-2 to cover broader pathogen diversity
- Metagenomic surveillance integrated into airport and border biosurveillance programs in high-travel-volume jurisdictions
- Clinical mNGS achieving broader clinical adoption for defined high-value use cases (CNS infections in immunocompromised) with established reimbursement pathways

**Longer-term (7-10 years) — Global integration and pandemic preparedness:**
- Functional global biosurveillance network providing near-real-time pathogen genomic intelligence across human-animal-environmental surveillance domains
- Metagenomic-based detection contributing to the "100 Days Mission" — WHO/CEPI goal of having safe, effective vaccines within 100 days of any pandemic pathogen identification (requires rapid pathogen characterization from metagenomics)
- AMR surveillance fully integrated with clinical antibiotic stewardship programs in high-income settings; capacity development programs enabling basic genomic AMR surveillance in low-income countries

**Key barriers to this trajectory:**

1. **Funding sustainability:** Post-pandemic surveillance funding is declining in many high-income countries despite demonstrated value — the endemic "surveillance valley of death" where surveillance investments are cut between pandemics remains the primary structural challenge.

2. **Laboratory and bioinformatics workforce capacity:** Building and maintaining the specialized expertise required for metagenomic surveillance analysis is severely limited in most settings — particularly in the low-income, high-spillover-risk countries where investment is most epidemiologically justified.

3. **Data sharing governance:** International data sharing frameworks (Nagoya Protocol, PABS, GISAID governance) remain contested and incomplete — creating practical barriers to the global genomic surveillance network that the pandemic demonstrated as essential.

4. **Regulatory pathway for clinical mNGS:** Reimbursement and regulatory approval for clinical mNGS in most healthcare systems remains nascent — without clear reimbursement pathways, clinical adoption will remain limited regardless of demonstrated diagnostic value.

**Enabling conditions:**

1. **COVID-19 infrastructure institutionalization:** Formal integration of pandemic-era wastewater and genomic surveillance infrastructure into core public health law and budget processes (currently occurring in some jurisdictions) provides durable funding independent of emergency declarations.

2. **Technology cost reduction:** Continued sequencing cost reduction (approaching $10/genome for short-read sequencing) and automation reducing labor costs make population-scale genomic surveillance increasingly cost-competitive with conventional surveillance methods.

3. **International framework agreement:** Resolution of PABS/GISAID governance debates enabling stable, equitable international sequence sharing would remove a major barrier to global surveillance network function.

4. **Pandemic Accord implementation:** The WHO Pandemic Accord (in final negotiation as of 2026) including provisions for surveillance data sharing and equitable access would provide an international legal framework supporting sustainable global surveillance.

## Summary
Metagenomic biosurveillance's integration into routine public health infrastructure is on a clear and supported trajectory — consolidating COVID-19 pandemic investments into durable programs, expanding pathogen portfolios beyond SARS-CoV-2, and progressively building toward a global One Health surveillance network — but requires sustained funding, bioinformatics workforce development, resolved international data-sharing governance, and explicit capacity-building investments in low-income, high-spillover-risk settings to realize its full potential for preventing rather than merely responding to future pandemic threats.
